Similar to a normal wave, matter waves have characterizing properties:

- wavelength
- frequency
- speed
- amplitude
# Wavelength

In 1929, Louis de Broglie won the Nobel prize for establishing the following relationship between the wavelength and momentum of a matter wave:

$$
\lambda=\frac{h}{p}
$$

For this reason, the wavelength of a matter wave is often called its **de Broglie wavelength**.

Applying this formula to large objects, such as a plane, shows why it's practically impossible to observe a wave-like behavior in large objects, since its wavelength is much too small.

# Frequency

The relationship for the frequency of a matter wave is the same as for electromagnetic radiation:

$$
f=\frac{E}{h}
$$

# Fundamental Wave-particle Relationships

For convenience, we express the two above equations in terms of the **wave number** and **angular frequency**:

- Wave number $k$, analogous to $\lambda$, $k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$
- Angular frequency $\omega$, analogous to $f$, $\omega=\frac{2\pi}{T}=2\pi f$
We make another convenient definition:

$$
\hbar=\frac{h}{2\pi}=\pu{ 1.055e-34 J*s }
$$

With these definitions, we establish the following fundamental wave-particle relationships:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p=\hbar k\\ \\

E=\hbar \omega
\end{aligned}
$$

# Amplitude

The amplitude of a wave, function of position and time, is called the wave function. The wave function of a matter wave, or probability amplitude, is referred to as $\Psi(x,t)$. Determining the wave function of a matter wave is a problem resolved by finding an appropriate solution to [[Schrödinger's equation]], which is obeyed by all matter waves.
