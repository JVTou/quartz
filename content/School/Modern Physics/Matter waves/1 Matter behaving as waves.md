One of the most widely known facts in physics is that electrons orbiting an atom can only have certain energies. Similarly to how a sound wave confined to an organ pipe, or a wave on a string can only take certain discrete standing waves, electrons confined to very small dimensions by an atom can only take on certain energetic values, i.e. the electrons are behaving like standing waves.  The atom confines the electrons to a certain small domain, smaller than its wavelength, which forces it to exhibit a wave-like nature.

## Slit Experiments

The wave-like nature of the electron is further shown through slit experiments, where the behavior of an electron beam after passing through a slit shows wave-like behavior.

### Single Slit Experiment

A beam of electrons, all with the same energy, passing through a single "wide" slit behaves as would be expected, landing with the same shape on a screen as the original beam.

![[Wide Slit]]

However, narrowing this slit expands the footprint left on the screen, similar to how a wave would develop after passing through an opening between two obstacles.

![[Narrow slit]]

This behavior is difficult to reconcile with the concept of electrons as particles. *Strange…*

### Double Slit Experiment

Adding a second narrow slit to the path of our monoenergetic electron beam results in a regularly spaced distribution of electrons on the screen, typical of two waves interfering, alternating constructive and destructive action.

![[Double slit]]

# The Bragg Law

In 1915, W.H. Bragg and his son W.L. Bragg won the Nobel prize for their work in diffraction of X-rays across crystals. In crystals, the atoms are regularly spaced such that they form layers. The spacing *d* between atoms is much smaller than the wavelength of an X-ray, such that passing through a layer of atoms will scatter. The Bragg law states that the waves scattering from each plane will form constructive interference at source angle $\theta$ from the crystal plane when:

$$
2d\sin\theta=m\lambda
$$

Effectively, after diffracting off the crystal's atomic layers, the X-rays reach a detector which reveals an interference pattern.

![[Crystal interference pattern]]

Similarly, we can find diffraction patterns in electrons, neutrons and even atoms if the dimensions are small enough. Davisson and Germer, as well as Sewell and Cohen developed techniques for electron microscopes using electron diffraction, respectively LEED (Low-energy electron diffraction) and RHEED (Reflection high-energy electron diffraction).

___

These experiments opens the door to the fact that massive objects can have a certain wavelength. We call these waves "[[Matter waves]]". They have [[2 Properties of matter waves|properties]], like amplitude, frequency and wavelength just like normal waves.

It is a common misconception that it is the mass itself that oscillates. Rather, it is the probability that we will find the object at that location that is oscillating.
